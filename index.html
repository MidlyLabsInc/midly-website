The problem

Contracts don’t stall in the drafting. They stall in the gaps.

Most of the delay around a commercial contract has nothing to do with the clauses. It is the work of finding out what happened, who decided it, and which draft is real.

  1. Legal hears about it last.

    The deal has a close date before anyone in legal has seen the paper.

  2. Nobody is sure which version is current.

    Three drafts in three inboxes, and a markup made against the wrong one.

  3. Signed, filed, forgotten.

    An executed agreement comes back out of the drawer once something has already gone wrong.

How Midly works

One connected contract lifecycle.

Each stage hands the next one everything it learned, so nothing is re-explained, re-found, or re-argued from nothing.

  1. 01

    Intake

    Forward contracts to your Midly intake inbox, or upload them, then file each one to the right client or matter. Tracked changes in Word files arrive intact.

  2. 02

    Review

    Every clause graded against your own playbook and anchored to the exact language, read from the side you are on.

  3. 03

    Negotiate

    Your edits go back as tracked changes in your name, with the argument beside the clause, and the discussion stays in one place.

  4. 04

    Store

    The executed agreement is kept with its key terms pulled out: dates, governing law, payment terms, and the liability cap.

  5. 05

    Monitor

    Expiry, auto-renewal and notice terms are pulled out, and Midly emails reminders before expiry and on the last day to give notice.

On the same agreement, the positions you took last round come with you into the next review. Findings are marked new, carried forward, or resolved, so a round does not start from nothing.

A real review

What Midly found in a vendor’s own paper.

We ran a SaaS vendor’s published master subscription agreement through Midly, from the customer’s side, against a customer-side playbook. It returned 14 findings. This is the one it marked red, exactly as Midly wrote it, with the names removed.

Section 22.8, with Midly’s suggested revision

Their clause, and the change Midly proposed

[Vendor] may modify this Agreement at any time by posting a revised version at [the vendor's website], which modifications will become effective as of the first day of the calendar month following the month in which they were first posted; provided, however, that if an Order specifies a fixed term of 12 months or longer, the modifications will instead be effective immediately upon the start of the next Renewal Order Term. In either case, if Customer objects to the updated Agreement, as its sole and exclusive remedy, Customer may choose not to renew in accordance with Section 6.3. and providing Customer with at least sixty (60) days' prior written notice of any material modification. Material modifications shall not become effective with respect to any then-current Order Term without Customer's prior written consent. If Customer objects to any material modification, Customer may terminate the applicable Order(s) upon written notice to [Vendor] within thirty (30) days of receiving notice of such modification, and [Vendor] will issue a Pro-Rated Refund for any prepaid, unused Fees. For the avoidance of doubt, any Order is subject to the version of the Agreement in effect at the time of the Order.

Struck is their text Midly would remove. Underlined is what it would add.

Unilateral Agreement Modification RightRed · Playbook deviation

The finding

Section 22.8 permits [Vendor] to modify this Agreement at any time by posting a revised version online, with changes becoming effective as early as the first day of the following calendar month. As the customer buying this subscription, [the customer] has no right to object and negotiate: the sole remedy is non-renewal under Section 6.3. This means [Vendor] can unilaterally alter payment terms, data rights, liability caps, or any other provision mid-relationship without [the customer's] consent. The playbook is explicit that no unilateral amendment rights are acceptable, making this a hard deviation. The practical exposure for a small firm is that a material change to, say, the data processing terms or the liability cap could take effect with as little as one month's notice and no ability to push back short of walking away from the platform.

The note Midly drafted for the other side

I accept that [Vendor] needs the ability to update its standard terms over time. What my client cannot accept is a unilateral right to modify material terms, including pricing, liability, and data rights, with no consent mechanism and no remedy beyond non-renewal. I propose a sixty-day advance notice requirement for material changes, with a right to terminate and receive a pro-rated refund if my client objects. This is a standard accommodation for enterprise SaaS customers and does not prevent [Vendor] from updating non-material operational terms on its current timeline.

Review standards Whatever you remembered to paste into the prompt this time. Your playbook, read from the side you represent. Red deviations stay flagged until someone resolves or acknowledges them.
